The Loud Lie Economy: Why Exaggeration Goes Viral and Truth Doesn't

January 15, 2026

Sam Levine’s essay, "Obvious Numbers, Viral Claims: AI, Water, and Media in a Gen Z Context," tackles a fundamental modern problem: how a ridiculous claim becomes an accepted fact[cite: 2]. Levine focuses on the viral lie that artificial intelligence uses so much water it could "fill the oceans"[cite: 3]. The accuracy of the claim isn't the point; the claim works because it's a perfect performance designed to grab attention[cite: 4].

Levine uses the term "attention currency" to explain this dynamic, connecting it to Michael Goldhaber's 1997 paper, "The Attention Economy and the Net"[cite: 5]. This framework shows that the truth is often too expensive—in terms of losing the spotlight—for anyone who wants people to pay attention to what they say[cite: 6].

The Goldhaber Rule: Attention is the New Oil

Goldhaber argued that in a world saturated with information, human attention is the only truly rare and valuable commodity[cite: 8]. This shift moves us from a cash-based economy to an attention economy, where attention is the actual source of power and wealth[cite: 9]. As Levine notes, "AI water claims act as attention currency, a way to participate in digital discourse without doing the deeper work of analysis"[cite: 12].

The Cost of Being Right

This economic shift actively punishes accuracy[cite: 18]. To earn this "currency," one must be louder, more shocking, and sound more certain than anyone else[cite: 19]. For writers obsessed with metrics, the burning need for currency often crushes the simple duty to be correct[cite: 21]. The choice is stark: be accurate and be invisible, or be loud and be wrong[cite: 22].

The Rebellion of Fact

Levine suggests using digital tools to "support judgment rather than replace it"[cite: 40]. By checking the difference between the "2 milliliters" and the "ocean," the writer consciously chooses to spend their attention on accuracy instead of chasing viral fame[cite: 41]. As I follow discussions on the Blog Network Dashboard, I am reminded that the future of honest writing depends entirely on the courage to be quiet[cite: 53, 59]. The truth might not go viral, but it is the only thing that lasts[cite: 54].
