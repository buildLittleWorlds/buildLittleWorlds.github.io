The Wealth of Hyperbole: "Attention Currency" and the Architecture of Viral Truth

January 15, 2026

In their essay “Obvious Numbers, Viral Claims: AI, Water, and Media in a Gen Z Context,” Sam Levine explores a modern epistemological crisis: the transformation of questionable data into "obvious" truth. Levine’s analysis centers on a specific instance of rhetorical inflation—the claim that artificial intelligence consumes enough water to "fill the oceans." While this figure is empirically indefensible, Levine argues that its factual inaccuracy is secondary to its performance. To explain this, they utilize a hauntingly precise phrase: "attention currency." By situating this term within the foundational theories of Michael Goldhaber, specifically his 1997 work “The Attention Economy and the Net,” we can see that Levine is not merely describing a bad habit of Gen Z media consumption, but rather identifying the fundamental economic logic of the digital age—one where the truth is a luxury we often cannot afford to "spend" our limited focus on.

To understand Levine’s "attention currency," one must first look to Goldhaber’s treatise. His central thesis was that in an "information-rich world," information itself ceases to be valuable because it is overabundant. Instead, the only remaining scarce resource is human attention. Goldhaber argued that we were transitioning away from a money-based economy toward an attention economy. In this new system, attention is not just a metaphor for interest; it is a literal currency that provides "the power to bend minds and bodies."

Levine applies this economic lens to the specific environmental myths surrounding AI. When a social media user or a content creator shares the claim that AI is "draining the oceans," they are not engaged in an act of scientific reporting; they are making a transaction. As Levine notes, "AI water claims act as attention currency, a way to participate in digital discourse without doing the deeper work of analysis." In Goldhaber’s terms, the person sharing the myth is attempting to accumulate "wealth" in the form of visibility, likes, and shares. Because the "ocean" metaphor is "emotionally striking" and "morally clear," it has a high exchange value. A more accurate statement—that a ChatGPT query uses roughly two milliliters of water—is, by contrast, economically worthless. It is too small to grab the eye and too nuanced to trigger the outrage necessary to circulate.

The tragedy of this economic shift, which Levine highlights through the lens of Gen Z writing practices, is that it creates a systemic disincentive for accuracy. Goldhaber pointed out that "the assembled audience cannot really pay attention to very many people speaking at once." This scarcity creates a brutal competition. To win the "currency" of attention, one must be louder, more shocking, and more certain than the competition. Levine observes this play out in the way "viral truths" are internalized. For a Gen Z writer operating within "networked publics," the pressure to be relevant—to have currency—often overrides the intellectual duty to be correct.

Levine’s use of "attention currency" also sheds light on the social hierarchy of the modern media landscape. Goldhaber predicted a new class division: "stars vs. fans." In Levine's context, this division manifests in the way individuals "signal virtue" by repeating inflated claims. By sharing the "ocean in the chatbot" myth, a user pays attention to a "star" (the original creator of the claim) while hoping to bank some of that attention for themselves. The truth of the water usage becomes irrelevant because the "property" being traded is the visibility of the post itself. The factual "bottle of water per query" is a leaden coin in a market that only accepts the gold of catastrophe.

Furthermore, Levine’s essay emphasizes the "problem of obviousness"—the moment a claim has been traded so many times that it becomes part of the cultural furniture. Goldhaber’s theory provides the "why" behind this phenomenon. He noted that attention-getting strategies "evolve in response to algorithms." If an algorithm rewards engagement, and engagement is driven by outrage, then the "truth" will naturally evolve to be as outrageous as possible. Over time, the repeated exposure to these high-value, high-outrage claims makes them feel "obvious." Levine warns that this "obviousness" is a trap for writers; it is the point where the currency has become so inflated that we no longer recognize we are trading in fiction.

However, Levine does not stop at diagnosis. They use the "attention currency" framework to propose a new form of intellectual labor. If the attention economy rewards the "allure of easy answers," then the act of "resisting viral drama" is an act of economic subversion. Levine suggests that using AI and digital tools "thoughtfully" can "support judgment rather than replace it." This is a direct challenge to the Goldhaber-esque world where we are merely "thralls" to whoever holds the most attention. By slowing down to verify the "2 milliliters" vs. the "ocean," the writer is choosing to spend their attention on accuracy rather than on the pursuit of viral wealth.

Ultimately, zooming in on the "attention currency" in Sam Levine’s essay reveals a grim reality of modern discourse: we are living in a world where the cost of being right is often social invisibility. Levine and Goldhaber together show that the "ocean" is not in the chatbot, but it is in our media environment—a vast, churning sea of claims where the only way to stay afloat is to grab onto the biggest, most buoyant lie available. Levine’s essay is a call for a new kind of "intellectual responsibility" that recognizes the value of the small, the quiet, and the true. It is an invitation to stop treating our attention as a currency to be spent on the loudest bidder and to start treating it as a sacred resource for understanding the world as it actually is.

The lesson for the writer is clear: to maintain one's integrity, one must be willing to be "poor" in the attention economy. One must be willing to trade the "ocean" for the "two milliliters," choosing the weight of evidence over the flight of the viral.
