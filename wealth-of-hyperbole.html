The Wealth of Hyperbole: "Attention Currency" and the Architecture of Viral Truth

January 15, 2026

In their essay “Obvious Numbers, Viral Claims: AI, Water, and Media in a Gen Z Context,” Sam Levine explores a modern epistemological crisis: the transformation of questionable data into "obvious" truth. Levine’s analysis centers on a specific instance of rhetorical inflation—the claim that artificial intelligence consumes enough water to "fill the oceans."

While this figure is empirically indefensible, Levine argues that its factual inaccuracy is secondary to its performance. To explain this, they utilize a hauntingly precise phrase: "attention currency." By situating this term within the foundational theories of Michael Goldhaber, specifically his 1997 work “The Attention Economy and the Net,” we can see that Levine is identifying the fundamental economic logic of the digital age—one where the truth is a luxury we often cannot afford to "spend" our limited focus on.

Goldhaber’s central thesis was that in an "information-rich world," information itself ceases to be valuable because it is overabundant. Instead, the only remaining scarce resource is human attention. In this system, attention is a literal currency that provides "the power to bend minds and bodies."

Levine applies this economic lens to environmental myths. When a creator shares the claim that AI is "draining the oceans," they are making a transaction. As Levine notes, "AI water claims act as attention currency, a way to participate in digital discourse without doing the deeper work of analysis." Because the "ocean" metaphor is emotionally striking, it has a high exchange value. A more accurate statement—that a ChatGPT query uses roughly two milliliters of water—is economically worthless by comparison.

The tragedy of this shift is that it creates a systemic disincentive for accuracy. To win the "currency" of attention, one must be louder and more shocking. For a Gen Z writer operating within "networked publics," the pressure to have currency often overrides the intellectual duty to be correct.

Furthermore, Levine’s essay emphasizes the "problem of obviousness"—the moment a claim has been traded so many times that it becomes part of the cultural furniture. If an algorithm rewards engagement driven by outrage, then the "truth" will naturally evolve to be as outrageous as possible. Levine warns that this "obviousness" is a trap; it is the point where we no longer recognize we are trading in fiction.

However, Levine proposes a new form of intellectual labor. If the attention economy rewards easy answers, then "resisting viral drama" is an act of economic subversion. By slowing down to verify the "2 milliliters" vs. the "ocean," the writer chooses to spend their attention on accuracy rather than viral wealth.

Ultimately, this reveals a grim reality: the cost of being right is often social invisibility. Levine’s essay is a call for "intellectual responsibility" that recognizes the value of the small and the true. To maintain integrity, a writer must be willing to be "poor" in the attention economy, choosing the weight of evidence over the flight of the viral.
