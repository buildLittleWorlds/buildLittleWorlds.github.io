The Loud Lie Economy: Why Exaggeration Goes Viral and Truth Doesn't

January 15, 2026

Sam Levine’s essay, "Obvious Numbers, Viral Claims: AI, Water, and Media in a Gen Z Context," tackles a fundamental modern problem: how a ridiculous claim becomes an accepted fact. Levine focuses on the viral lie that artificial intelligence uses so much water it could "fill the oceans." The accuracy of the claim isn't the point; the claim works because it's a perfect performance designed to grab attention.

Levine uses the term "attention currency" to explain this dynamic, connecting it to Michael Goldhaber's 1997 paper, "The Attention Economy and the Net." This framework shows that the truth is often too expensive—in terms of losing the spotlight—for anyone who wants people to pay attention to what they say.

The Goldhaber Rule: Attention is the New Oil

Goldhaber argued that in a world saturated with information, human attention is the only truly rare and valuable commodity. This shift moves us from a cash-based economy to an attention economy, where attention is the actual source of power and wealth.

Levine applies this logic to viral environmental stories about AI. When someone posts the "AI draining the oceans" claim, they are making a trade. As Levine notes, "AI water claims act as attention currency, a way to participate in digital discourse without doing the deeper work of analysis." The sharer is seeking wealth in likes, shares, and visibility. The "ocean" metaphor is valuable because it is "emotionally striking" and "morally clear," instantly triggering anger or shock. The accurate number—a standard ChatGPT question uses about two milliliters of water—has zero market value. It's too small and complex to trigger the viral outburst needed.

The Cost of Being Right: Why Accuracy Fails

This economic shift actively punishes accuracy. Goldhaber observed that attention is a finite resource, leading to a vicious, winner-take-all fight for the spotlight. To earn this "currency," one must be louder, more shocking, and sound more certain than anyone else. Levine observes this in how "viral truths" become deeply rooted beliefs. For writers obsessed with metrics, the burning need for currency often crushes the simple duty to be correct. The choice is stark: be accurate and be invisible, or be loud and be wrong.

Levine’s "attention currency" also explains the social status game. Goldhaber predicted a new class system of "stars vs. fans." In this world, users "signal virtue" just by repeating massive lies. Sharing the "ocean in the chatbot" myth pays attention to a "star" while hoping to earn attention for oneself. The factual "two milliliters" is a heavy, useless piece of metal in a market that only values the shiny gold of disaster and panic.

The Trap of Obviousness

The need to exaggerate leads to the "problem of obviousness." A claim repeated and traded enough times stops being a shocking lie and becomes part of the cultural furniture. Goldhaber theorized that attention-grabbing methods "evolve in response to algorithms." Since algorithms reward outrage-driven engagement, the "truth" changes to become as outrageous as possible. Constantly seeing these high-value claims makes them feel "obvious." Levine warns this "obviousness" is a major danger; it’s the exact moment we forget we are trading in total fiction. Feeling "obvious" is a clear sign that the lie has won the market.

The Rebellion of Fact

Levine uses the "attention currency" framework to argue for a new kind of work. If the attention economy rewards "the allure of easy answers," then the simple act of "resisting viral drama" is a powerful act of economic protest. Levine suggests using digital tools to "support judgment rather than replace it." By checking the difference between the "2 milliliters" and the "ocean," the writer consciously chooses to spend their attention on accuracy instead of chasing viral fame.

Levine’s essay reveals a brutal reality: the price of being right is often total social invisibility. Levine and Goldhaber together prove that the "ocean" isn't in the chatbot; it is the media landscape—a vast, confused sea where the only way to stay afloat is to grab the biggest, most exciting lie. Levine demands a new kind of "intellectual responsibility," asking us to treat attention as a sacred, limited resource for figuring out the world.

The Writer’s New Rule

The message for every writer is simple: to keep your integrity, you must be okay with being "poor" in the attention economy. You must ditch the exciting "ocean" claim for the boring "two milliliters" fact, choosing solid evidence over the viral post. Real integrity means being ready to publish work that gets zero shares. This refusal to play the exaggeration game is the only way to build genuine trust, which is stable, long-term wealth—attention is just inflated paper. This demands putting the hard truth far ahead of the easy myth, and accepting that your best-researched work might only be read by a handful of people. Those few are the ones who matter, creating a counter-economy based on facts. The future of honest writing depends entirely on the courage to be quiet. The truth might not go viral, but it is the only thing that lasts.
